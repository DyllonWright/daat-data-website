Project Exodus

Somebody built this format fifty years ago and never published the numbers.

So we measured them. One hundred and three recordings, window by window, each ear separately: every tone pair, every level, every slope written down.

A guided meditation recording with binaural beats runs on a specification: two carrier tones, a few hertz apart, a voice at a set level above a noise bed. Fifty years of recordings exist in this format. The specification behind them never appeared in print.

Robert Monroe established the format between 1975 and 1995. The recordings themselves, their scripts, their manuals and their product names stay with their publisher, and this project copies none of them. The method sits in two expired patents and a declassified US Army assessment — public, and free to build on.

What nobody published, anyone with a spectrogram and enough patience could reconstruct. We spent the patience.

Fifty years of people describing the effect.
Nobody writing down the input.

That last row carries the weight. A renderer that lands within a hundredth of a hertz of its own script turns the specification from a description of someone else's recordings into something anyone can build to.

The claim, and the claim we refuse

These recordings carry binaural beats. The best available evidence does not support the claim that binaural beats entrain brainwaves — Ingendoh, Posny and Heine (2023), PLOS ONE 18(5): e0286023. We make no such claim.

We make one narrower claim instead, and publish the measurements that check it: each recording holds exactly the frequencies its description states.

Most of this field sells an effect it cannot evidence. Reading the literature honestly leaves one defensible position, and it happens to suit a measurement project: claim fidelity to a published specification, publish the specification, and let anyone with a spectrogram check the work. A listener gets recordings for relaxation and stress management, built to numbers they can verify, from a project that tells them what the research actually shows.

That reads as a smaller promise than the competition makes. It also reads as the only one this project can keep.

What's coming

The measurement finished. The ladder — how many steps, how far apart, what each one asks of a listener — sits drafted and unbuilt, and the layer that interests me most has no equivalent in the originals: a word the listener chooses themselves, conditioned during the recording, that later works on its own.

No date. No price. Nothing to join. The explainers come before any of it, because the measurement makes a better argument than a product page would.

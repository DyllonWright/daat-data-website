## Copy — "What this measures, and what it doesn't"

Plenty of good instruments already ask who you are. This one asks a different question, so
here sits the map before you start.

**Big Five (OCEAN)** measures traits: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion,
agreeableness, neuroticism. Decades of data behind it, replication across cultures, and
genuine predictive power for things like job performance. It answers *how do you tend to
behave?* If you want the empirically strongest picture of your dispositions, take that one.

**Myers-Briggs** gives teams and individuals a shared vocabulary for difference across four
preference pairs, and millions of people find it useful for exactly that. Academic psychology
raises reliability questions about sorting continuous distributions into types; the practical
value people report from it stays real regardless. It answers *how do you prefer to work and
decide?*

**The Enneagram** maps motivation — what drives a person and what they fear underneath the
driving. It does well in therapeutic and spiritual-direction settings where the question
concerns why someone keeps doing the thing they keep doing.

**Human Design, natal astrology, and similar systems** build a rich symbolic vocabulary from
birth data and give people a language for self-narrative. They answer *what symbolic pattern
can I read my life through?*

**This one asks none of those.** It measures **metaphysical commitments**: what you hold to
count as real, what you think actually changes a person, what you accept as authorizing a
claim, and whether you consider the world fundamentally trustworthy. Then it names the
tradition whose answers already match yours, and the tradition furthest from you.

The two kinds of question run independently of each other. Two people with nearly identical
Big Five profiles can hold opposite verdicts on whether the world functions as a school or as
a confinement, and that disagreement shapes their lives more than their trait scores do.

### What this does not claim

Saying this plainly matters more than any claim made for it:

- **No psychometric validation.** No reliability studies, no validity studies, no normed
  population. It has never been administered under research conditions and no number here
  carries a confidence interval.
- **Not a personality test.** It predicts nothing about behaviour, performance, compatibility
  or outcomes, and it should not inform a hiring decision, a diagnosis, or a relationship.
- **One reading of each tradition.** Seventeen positions carry coordinates assigned by a
  practitioner who studies them. A specialist in any one of them could argue every number,
  and some would.
- **Twenty questions is a small instrument.** It sorts; it does not measure finely.

### What it does that you can check

Every position names primary sources. Not summaries, not secondary commentary: the texts
themselves, with translator and edition, in versions a reader can actually obtain.

That produces a claim of a different kind from validation. A validated instrument asks you to
trust a statistical process you cannot personally inspect. This one asks you to trust
nothing: read the passage, and decide for yourself whether it describes where you already
stand. If the result misses, the text you read will tell you so faster than any score could.

Traceability instead of validation. Those two count as different virtues, and this
instrument only claims the second one.

### Why the questions force a choice

No middle option appears anywhere, on purpose. A neutral answer records nothing, and the
traditions the instrument sorts between do not sit neutral on these questions either.

Both options in every pair state a position somebody has held seriously and defended well. If
one of them ever reads as the obviously wiser answer, treat that as a defect in the question
rather than a test of your judgement, and say so.

### Why a low score reads as good news

The coherence score reports how consistently your own answers point one direction. A high
score describes a settled position. A low one describes friction, usually because two
frameworks you absorbed at different times disagree with each other and you have never had
cause to notice.

A low score makes you no worse. It makes the result more interesting, and it usually points
at something specific and findable.
